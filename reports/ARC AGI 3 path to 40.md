# Tuning Franzen's Notebook Buys Points, Not Forty

On Kaggle-only compute, the evidence supports a climb from our current ~26 to a **hidden mean of roughly 28–31**, with a best displayed public score in the low-to-mid 30s, by Nov 2. **Forty or more is a long shot. I put it at about 5% on the displayed board and 2–3% on the private board.** Forty requires roughly one more cleared level in every hidden game. No lever published by anyone running on the single RTX PRO 6000 has delivered that much. The only two teams above 45, Tufa Labs (52.51) and Yi-Chia Chen (48.07), have published nothing that explains their scores, and the best public recipe is still Franzen's 27.89. The cheapest remaining points are in memory and speed on the Franzen stack. His winning notebook left **4.25 GB of VRAM free**, carried a **3.79 GB speculative draft** that can shrink to about 1.5 GB, and ran lossless MTP acceptance. He also reports that scores were still rising at the end of the 9-hour run. Next comes solved-level memory, worth +2.8 in sirikilohit's score history. After that come computed evidence lines that attack wrong-goal loops; so far these have support only from frontier-model systems. The ARC-Scientist design picks the right target, wrong-goal loops and "thrashing", but the wrong tool. A closed mechanics library collides with a private set built to be out of distribution, and the frontier papers it cites say the bottleneck is goal inference, not dynamics. A goal-first, open-ended "lite" version can be built and gated by Oct 20, and its CPU measurements make a strong Paper Track entry whatever the leaderboard does. One hidden draw varies by ±2–3 points, and the public board scores only half the hidden games. The plan below therefore spends hidden draws only on changes that could plausibly move 3 or more points. It accepts throughput changes on mechanism evidence plus a no-harm check, and it selects final submissions by configuration mean rather than by best draw.

*How to read this report.* "Hidden" means the Kaggle public leaderboard. It is computed on about 55 of the 110 hidden games. "Private" means the other 55, which decide the final ranking. "Team data" means our own runs and census. **[Inference]** marks my reasoning rather than a sourced fact, and every probability is my estimate.

## Forty means one more cleared level in every hidden game

The scoring rule explains why the gap is so large. ARC-AGI-3 scores each level as min(1.15, (human actions / AI actions)²). It weights level *l* by *l*, gives uncompleted levels zero, and averages games ([Tech Report §4](https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf); [methodology](https://docs.arcprize.org/methodology)). Across the 25 public games (mean 7.32 levels), clearing the first *k* levels of every game at human efficiency yields at most **3.52 / 10.57 / 21.14 / 35.24 / 52.85** for *k* = 1 to 5 (team data). **[Inference]** On that ladder, our 25.78 corresponds to clearing about 3.3 levels everywhere, and 40 corresponds to about 4.3. Franzen's own demo run supports the reading: on 10 public games it averaged **4.0 cleared levels and 36.56 points**, while the same notebook scores 27.89 on the hidden board (team data; [Franzen WRITEUP](https://github.com/da-fr/arc-agi-3-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). **Reaching 40 on the hidden set therefore means doing better on hidden games than the best public notebook does on the easier demo games.** The organizers designed the private games to be "significantly more difficult" and "intentionally out-of-distribution relative to the public set" ([Tech Report §3.6](https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf)).

Depth dominates efficiency, which tells us where to aim. In our 25-game census of the older B81 notebook, perfect efficiency on the levels already cleared would have raised the public-25 score from 8.24 to 10.50. One more cleared level in every game would have raised it to **19.92**, about five times the gain (team data). That census also found **11 of 25 games stuck on a level that had already consumed more actions than a human needs for the whole level** (team data). Examples: vc33 at 264 actions against a 61-action baseline, sc25 at 254 against 32, and s5i5 at 218 against 89. **[Inference]** A late clear of a thrashed level is worth almost nothing on its own ((61/264)² ≈ 0.05). Its real value is that it unlocks the heavier later levels, and only if time remains. Thrashing costs time and depth, not just that level's score.

The jump from B81 to Franzen's notebook settles which kind of lever matters. B81 hidden draws ranged from **3.03 to 5.36**, and a long list of harness grafts on that chassis read as noise ([Thui MAP](https://github.com/Sahasawatt/arc-agi-3-agent/blob/master/notes/wayfinder/MAP.md)). Our prompt extras even lowered the mean, to 3.47 against B81's 4.19 (team data). Copying Franzen's notebook unchanged scored **25.78**. **[Inference]** A five-to-six-fold gain came from the serving stack, context length and perception, not from prompt work. That is consistent with Tufa Labs' Milestone-1 conclusion that "the main driver of improvement … came from better base models and introducing multi-modality" ([Kaggle discussion 717133](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/717133)). It is also why the 40 target is hard. The next model upgrade is not coming, and post-training the 125B Flash-Next does not fit a single RTX PRO 6000. The one competitor who built a Flash-Next RL pipeline needed a full H200 node and "did not gain any performance" ([discussion 742835](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/742835)).

The three open-sourced notebooks converge on one lesson, and that lesson should govern everything we build. What helped was **restoring evidence the agent loses or never saw**. Franzen called animation frames the strongest perception change. Other examples are the fatal frame after game over, UNDO, history kept long enough that the agent remembers what it tried, persistent model-written functions, and the pinned rule plus winning actions of each cleared level ([Franzen WRITEUP](https://github.com/da-fr/arc-agi-3-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md); [sirikilohit WRITEUP](https://github.com/LohitSiriki/arc-agi-3-milestone2-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). What hurt was **telling the agent what to do**. sirikilohit's added prompt text dropped his score from 6.42 to 4.16, and a level-start advice block cut it from 13.40 to 9.98. Franzen found that structured world-model updates, summarization, prescriptive verification hints and repeated-state guards gave no clear gain, and that disabling the world-model note was best once context was large. The model's own reasoning is the one piece of history nobody should trim. Franzen reports that removing it "leads to a large regression in score", and Thuitanium's B92 saw levels fall from 18 to 11 when stored reasoning was no longer re-sent ([Thui MAP B92](https://github.com/Sahasawatt/arc-agi-3-agent/blob/master/notes/wayfinder/MAP.md)). For the ARC-Scientist design, the implication is direct: **anything it shows the model must be a computed fact about the game, not a suggestion.**

What lifted the leaders from about 27 to about 50 in under a week is unknown. **[Inference]** Three explanations compete. The first is post-training. Tufa's Sep 9 forks of RL frameworks and Yi-Chia Chen's June distillation and speculative-draft work show both teams can do it, and Tufa's own lesson ranks model capability first. That path is closed to a Kaggle-only team. The second is a harness or paradigm change that a strong team found and has not shared. That path is open in principle, but we cannot see it. The third is best-of-many selection. With 153 entries and a per-draw spread of a few points, Tufa's displayed 52.5 overstates its expected private score by perhaps 3–5 points. That accounts for a fraction of the gap, not 20 points. The practical consequences are twofold. First, we should not expect to reverse-engineer the leaders. Second, public notebooks remain the one free source of a step change. Kaggle disables kernel publishing after **Oct 26** ([Kaggle overview](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/overview)), so any further public notebook will appear before then. Franzen and sirikilohit have each promised further full public-game runs. A daily scan of the Code tab is the cheapest action in this plan.

## Memory and speed are the cheapest remaining points

Franzen's own conclusion gives this section its warrant: "With scores still improving near the end of runs, increasing token throughput remained a promising way to improve the score" ([Franzen WRITEUP](https://github.com/da-fr/arc-agi-3-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). The one clean memory ablation in the field points the same way: sirikilohit's server logs showed the cache pool **91–97% full** all run, and FP8 KV plus a longer trim window added 8 points with prompts unchanged ([sirikilohit WRITEUP](https://github.com/LohitSiriki/arc-agi-3-milestone2-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). **[Inference]** Franzen's 10 streams at 59–118k tokens each hold 0.6–1.2M tokens of resident context against a 1.01M pool, so his pool is saturated too. At his measured footprint of about **11.45 KB per FP8 KV token** (11.58 GB for 1,011,264 tokens), every gigabyte freed buys roughly 87k tokens ([COMPARISON](https://github.com/tonghuikang/daniel-franzen-arc-agi-3/blob/main/kaggle/COMPARISON.md)).

**[Inference]** The first three rows together add about +500k tokens, taking the pool to roughly 1.5M. That is in line with Lord Han Solo's 1.42M, so it is achievable on this card. The open question is how to spend it. The evidence leans toward more aggregate tokens. The winner ran the fewest streams with the longest context, but Franzen names throughput, not context, as his remaining limiter. Adding streams raised peak aggregate decode by about 20% across the three prize notebooks. Twelve streams at Franzen's unchanged 118k → 59k trim policy need about 1.42M tokens at worst, which the enlarged pool covers. The alternative is ten streams with a higher drain floor, close to Lord Han Solo's 81,536. That is a reasonable second arm for local screening, but it should not get hidden draws of its own. Speed is the more speculative half. Relaxed acceptance changes the sampling distribution, and Franzen deliberately ran lossless. Online FP8 is proven only on a checkpoint Franzen moved away from, and sirikilohit's closest attempt made decoding slower. Both deserve a one-hour bench, not a hidden draw, until they show a speed gain with no loss in levels per generated token.

Several levers are closed and should not cost quota. Drafts deeper than 4 tokens are refused because Qwen's sparse attention caps draft tokens at its compress ratio, and `torch.compile` capture fails ([Olympie PERF_CEILING](https://github.com/gabrielolympie/sglang-flashnext-sm120/blob/main/docs/PERF_CEILING.md)). Pruning 25% of experts gave "more room, worse play". Temperature 1.0 against 0.6 showed nothing. Restarting a stuck level with fresh context "bought nothing" ([sirikilohit WRITEUP](https://github.com/LohitSiriki/arc-agi-3-milestone2-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). Image resolution is contested (Franzen found 10× best, sirikilohit found higher resolution no help) and not worth a hidden draw. Scheduling is the subtlest closed door. Franzen's priority term already decays a game's priority with actions and tokens spent on the current level, via the factor C = 0.25·2^(−(a/115)²) + 0.75·2^(−(t/62000)²), calibrated on 725 level attempts ([Franzen WRITEUP](https://github.com/da-fr/arc-agi-3-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). The time cost of thrashing is therefore already partly managed. What no scheduler can fix is an agent that cannot escape its wrong goal, and that is a harness problem. **[Inference]** If the pool and speed levers land, I expect them to add **+1 to +3 hidden points**. That is not enough for 40, but it is the highest-confidence gain available.

## ARC-Scientist is right about the target, wrong about the tool

The design's diagnosis is well supported. Wrong-goal loops are Tufa's named failure mode. Our census measured them as thrashing in 11 of 25 games. ARC Prize's analysis of frontier models lists them as "true local effect, false world model", genre mapping, and "solved the level, didn't learn the game" ([ARC Prize analysis](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-agi-3-gpt-5-5-opus-4-7-analysis)). The remedy also matches the frontier. Every top public-set system writes an executable world model and replays it against all past transitions before acting. **Twin clears 179 of 183 public levels and scores 93.3, against 61.1 for the same model in an off-the-shelf harness** ([Twin](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14490)). Kepler reaches 100 RHAE with Claude Opus 5 ([Kepler](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00834)), and EWM fully solves 15 of 25 games with GPT-5.5 ([EWM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05138)). The design's "evidence, not advice" principle is exactly the M2 winners' lesson. Its "killer property", that coverage and planner success can be measured on CPU before spending GPU hours, is real and valuable.

Five problems stand between the design and a leaderboard gain. **First, a closed library of public-game mechanics runs against how the private set was built.** The private games have "limited overlap with the mechanics found in the public environments". The organizers' novelty test rejects any pair of games solvable by one program at least 50% shorter than the two solution programs concatenated. Each game must combine multiple mechanics, and later levels deliberately add new pieces ([Tech Report §3.4–3.6](https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf)). **[Inference]** A library drawn from the 12 recurring primitive families in the public games could express the core level 1–3 dynamics of perhaps 18–22 of the 25 games. It would cover all-level dynamics for only 10–15, and well under half of the private games. The misses fall on the heavily weighted later levels. **Second, dynamics is not the bottleneck.** Twin's authors write that "building a usable world model is simpler than anticipated, whereas the harder problem is inferring the right goal." Its worst failures were goals: sc25, where a hidden countdown consumed 647 of 701 actions on one level, and sp80's sixth level, whose goal was never found despite 92.3% dynamics accuracy ([Twin](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14490)).

**Third, small-model evidence on simulator-writing is uniformly negative so far.** Thuitanium's executable-world-model smoke cut the action rate to 0.95, 0.48 and 0.24 of control, and `verify_world_model()` was called once. A peer's controller-owned hypothesis test found that only **5 of 33** small-model hypotheses compiled faithfully ([Thui MAP B82, B98](https://github.com/Sahasawatt/arc-agi-3-agent/blob/master/notes/wayfinder/MAP.md)). Offered goto tools and a physics planner were null ([tantan graft inventory](https://github.com/tantan0327/arc-agi-3-agent/blob/main/docs/graft-inventory.md)). Tufa found hand-built tools "actually hurt the model" ([Milestone 1 blog](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-prize-2026-milestone-1)). **Fourth, the throughput argument is weak.** Reasoning, not code, dominates generated tokens, and the reasoning is load-bearing. Shrinking the code the model writes saves little. **Fifth, the timeline does not fit the statistics.** The design reaches its first integrated test on Oct 20. That leaves about 13 draws, which cannot confirm any gain under about 4 points (see the noise section). The design's own estimate of "0 to +5 if it integrates in time" is honest, but integration by Oct 20 is itself uncertain.

The adjustment is to invert the design. It becomes **goal-first, open-ended, and limited to one computed line**, and its parts are reordered by evidence and cost.

**Ship the level manual first.** Port sirikilohit's solved-level memory, which pins the model's own rule plus the last 30 winning actions of each cleared level so they survive context trims. It is "the only text addition that helped" (+2.8 on his board) ([sirikilohit WRITEUP](https://github.com/LohitSiriki/arc-agi-3-milestone2-solution/blob/main/WRITEUP.md)). Agno's manuals carried across levels point the same way ([Agno](https://www.agno.com/articles/arc-agi-arcade)). **[Inference]** Franzen already keeps history, functions and "level-transfer guidance", so the overlap is large and the expected gain is 0 to +1.5. It is still the cheapest part.

**Make goal inference the core, not dynamics.** Twin's pre-reward goal finder ranks candidates by five visual signals (colour gone, colour new, local burst, big change, frontier). A goal hypothesis must be false on every recorded non-win frame, and a goal reached without a win is excluded for good. The first hypothesis was correct on **156 of 179 levels** ([Twin](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14490)). This is pure CPU code and needs no LLM. It attacks wrong-goal loops directly by falsifying them. The design's typed goal templates belong here: coverage or match-to-reference, reach-exit, all-targets-filled, sequence-equals-reference, and exact count.

**Add latent-state templates.** Every public game shows a budget bar ([arc-explainer](https://github.com/82deutschmark/arc-explainer/tree/main/shared/arc3Games)), and hidden counters, lives, timers and history-dependent agents are where frontier systems fail. A counter or timer template fitted to the HUD bar is cheap supervision for exactly the sc25-type failure.

**Keep the hypothesis language open.** The hypothesis should be the model's own persisted `predict()` in Python, which Franzen's sandbox already keeps for the whole game. The design's primitives become importable helper functions, not a closed catalogue. CPU enumeration is limited to object-level rules: which object moves under which action, what blocks it, and which counter ticks. These are core-knowledge physics the tech report says all games share.

**Show one gated, factual line.** Examples: "goal 'all yellow covered' falsified at step 41", or "your predict() matches 41/45 transitions, first mismatch at step 17". The line appears only when the verifier's recent accuracy passes a bar, such as our affordance graft's rule of ≥80% over the last 30 actions and beating the majority label (team data). It never gives an instruction.

**Make information-gain probes targeted, never blind.** When two surviving hypotheses disagree on an action's outcome, name that action. Blind probing failed outright: 0 of 104 stalls cleared and 0 level-ups in 300 presses ([Thui MAP B85](https://github.com/Sahasawatt/arc-agi-3-agent/blob/master/notes/wayfinder/MAP.md)). A targeted probe costs a handful of actions against the hundreds a thrash burns.

**Build the planner last and make it opt-in.** A `bfs(step_fn, goal_fn)` callable from the sandbox, CPU-capped near Twin's depth 8 and 20k nodes, is used only where `predict()` verifies. Depth is worth about five times efficiency in our census, so the planner is the lowest-value part for score.

The revised build has hard gates. **By Oct 6**, on CPU, measure goal-signal falsification accuracy and object-level primitive coverage. Develop on 12 public games and report on the other 13. Also report on NVIDIA's 25 Apache-2.0 DreamTeam games as an out-of-distribution proxy ([NVIDIA/dream-team](https://github.com/NVIDIA/dream-team)). In parallel, read Franzen transcripts to count how often Flash-Next already writes a predict-like function. **By Oct 12**, run the line behind a flag on recorded transcripts and in paired local runs. **By Oct 19**, it enters the leaderboard build only if three conditions hold: a verified `predict()` or a surviving goal hypothesis exists in at least 30% of games (Thuitanium's B98 availability bar), throughput loss is at most 5%, and paired local runs show no loss in levels per million tokens. **[Inference]** I estimate a 30% chance that it ships in the final build. If it ships, the hidden effect is −1 to +3. Its Paper Track value is high either way. "What fraction of ARC-AGI-3 transitions and goals a core-knowledge primitive set explains, on held-out games, and how often a small offline model's simulator verifies" is novel, measurable and honest. The Paper Track's six criteria include **Universality and Novelty** ([Paper Track](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-paper-track)). A held-out protocol protects Universality, and the tech report warns that ARC-specific harness strategies count as domain-specific overfitting for the official board ([Tech Report §4.3](https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf)).

## Noise decides ranks inside the 25–30 crowd

The instrument is noisier than the effects we can hope to buy. Four noise sources compound, and each has a measured magnitude.

The RESET episode is our best calibration point. A change the agent almost never exercised produced a 7-point swing on a 10-game demo. **[Inference]** A 10-game demo run can therefore screen for crashes, throughput and mechanism, but not for score. Paired 25-game runs that compare levels per million generated tokens, as sirikilohit recommends, are the minimum local evidence. The same arithmetic limits hidden testing. At a per-draw sd of 2.5, detecting a 3-point effect at a lenient one-sided 10% level with 80% power takes about **6 draws per arm**. A 2-point effect takes about 14 per arm. About 31 submission days remain until Nov 2, so the budget supports **two decision A/Bs of ≥3-point effects**. It cannot resolve any 1–2-point lever. That is why the plan accepts serving changes on mechanism evidence (pool size, tokens generated, cache reuse, zero OOM) plus a no-harm check on hidden draws. Franzen's statement that throughput converts into score is the prior that justifies it.

Shake-up risk has two layers. **[Inference]** For our own score, the private half can land 4–5 points either side of the public half on game sampling alone. The most likely direction is down, because the best displayed draw is selected on public noise. For our rank, the bigger risk is crowding. Franzen's notebook has been public since Sep 30, and any team can submit it, so the 24–30 band will fill with near-copies. Copies share the same game split, so they differ only by run noise of about 2–3 points. Rank inside that band is close to a lottery unless a real gain separates us from it. The host has said private scores are computed at original run time and never rerun ([Thui MAP B95, quoting the host](https://github.com/Sahasawatt/arc-agi-3-agent/blob/master/notes/wayfinder/MAP.md)). Final selection therefore cannot improve a run, only choose among runs. Two rules follow. **Choose configurations by their mean over draws, never by their single best draw.** Within a configuration, prefer runs whose logs show no timeouts, OOMs or stalls, because run-level failures hit both halves. Then select one **safe** build (highest mean with five or more clean draws) and one **ambitious** build (best mean with three or more draws, only if that mean is not below the safe build's).

## Conclusion

The step from 4 to 26 came from copying a better chassis. The step from 26 to 40 cannot come that way, because no better public chassis exists and the leaders who have one are not sharing it. Points will come from feeding the existing model more tokens and more of its own evidence, which is worth a few points with high confidence. A real jump would need an agent that stops chasing wrong goals. The ARC-Scientist idea earns its place there, but only after it is turned around. Its value lies in falsifying goals and surfacing latent counters with computed facts, not in cataloguing public-game mechanics that the private set was built to avoid.

The deeper lesson is about measurement. With one draw a day, ±2–3 points of run noise and a board that scores half the games, this competition rewards teams that can afford hundreds of evaluations. Tufa's 153 entries are part of its edge. A Kaggle-only team cannot buy that, but it can decline to be fooled by noise. It can gate on mechanisms, test only large effects, select on means, and publish what the instrument can and cannot resolve. That discipline protects the leaderboard result, and it is also the most credible contribution this team can make to the Paper Track.
